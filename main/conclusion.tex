\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:discussion}



We present \tool, a verifier that accelerates constraint-solver-based
global robustness verification and is the first to support its
incremental verification. \tool tightens the encoding with
perturbation-difference intervals and zonotope bounds, relaxes neurons
with a novel conditional-triangle relaxation, and, in transfer mode,
reuses a prior verification through difference bounds and warm-start
hints. \tool speeds up \baseline by $7.6\times$ on
average, and by \dremove{$12.4$}\dadd{$12.5$}$\times$ with transfer, on the instances
where at least one verifier terminates. On the
hardest tasks, where all verifiers time out, it returns bounds
$19.9\times$ tighter, and $20.9\times$
with transfer. Finally, our results show that the gains of \tool carry
over to larger networks, a network with
over $3\times$ more neurons than the largest network of \baseline and a
network with 500 neurons in one hidden layer, on which the bounds of
\baseline remain over $97\%$ of $\delta_{\max}$ apart at the timeout. We identify several future directions.

\section{Small Perturbations}
\label{sec:future-small-perturbations}

\dremove{A small perturbation is not necessarily an easy one. Once a perturbation of any type is small enough, the two copies nearly coincide. \tool profits from this far less than it might: its per-neuron bounds are taken over the whole input domain, so the number of unstable neurons, and hence of binary variables, does not fall with the size of the perturbation. Moreover, existing work still encodes the network twice, even when the perturbed copy is nearly identical to the clean one. That closeness can be exploited instead.}
\dadd{The first direction focuses on small perturbations, such as a brightness
shift of $\ep{=}0.000001$. While this might seem simpler, it is not,
since \tool's per-neuron bounds are taken over the whole input domain
and do not tighten with~$\ep$, so the number of unstable neurons, and
hence of binary variables, does not fall with the size of the
perturbation. However, once a perturbation of any type is small enough,
the two copies nearly coincide, and existing work still encodes the
network twice, even when the perturbed copy is nearly identical to the
clean one. As a future direction, one can exploit this closeness:} once
the difference intervals are tight enough to stand in for the perturbed
copy, \tool can encode the clean copy alone and replace each perturbed
neuron by its difference interval. The program then carries binary variables for one copy rather
than two. The condition this leaves is necessary for
$\conf{N}{x^{p}}{c_t}>0$ but not sufficient, so it over-approximates the
feasible pairs. The upper bound $\dup$ therefore remains sound, and how
much precision this costs for a small $\ep$ is left for future research.
\section{\dremove{Verifying New Perturbations in Global Robustness Settings}\dadd{Supporting New Perturbation Types}}
\label{sec:future-perturbations}

\tool, and the verifiers we compare it to, support only a handful of
perturbations. Perturbations that constrain all coordinates jointly,
rather than one at a time, are yet to be explored in these settings:
$\ell_0$, $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$. Each of them imposes a single budget
that all coordinates draw on. For $\ell_0$ it is the number of changed
coordinates, for $\ell_1$ the sum of the changes, and for $\ell_2$ the
sum of their squares. \tool propagates the perturbation coordinate by
coordinate, which discards this budget, since any single coordinate may
consume all of it. The difficulty is in the propagation. It carries each
neuron's difference separately, so two neurons may both sit at their
worst case although no perturbation within the budget can place them
there. The zonotope cannot represent the budget either, as it is a
linear image of a box. Carrying one shared budget through a network
whose abstractions are built to carry a box is what this direction
needs. Each of these perturbations has been addressed in the local
robustness setting~\citep{shapira2023calzone, croce2020lp,
cohen2019smoothing}. However, it is yet to be explored how to adapt such
approaches to a setting with two copies and an unknown input, or whether
new approaches are needed altogether. We therefore propose to pursue
this direction in future work.

\section{Training Networks for Verifiability}
\label{sec:future-training}

Every direction above takes the network as given. The cost of the
analysis, however, is set by the network. It is exponential in the
number of unstable neurons, which is fixed by bounds taken over the
whole input domain, regardless of the perturbation. If a network can be
trained so that few of its neurons are unstable, the complexity of the
analysis falls, and with it the verification time. Training for
verifiability is being studied for local robustness, by regularizing
pre-activation bounds that straddle zero~\citep{xiao2019relustability}
and by replacing unstable neurons with retrained linear
functions~\citep{chen2022grafting}. In the global setting, GloRo
nets~\citep{leino2021gloro} train for a Lipschitz-based notion of
robustness with built-in certification. Training for the global
robustness that \tool verifies is yet to be studied. \tool, moreover,
asks for less than stability. It needs only the normalized triangle area
to be small, which is continuous where the sign is not, and is what the
relaxation actually tests. Full stability over the whole domain would
leave an affine map, so the target is a frontier rather than a goal: how
many neurons can be \dremove{decided}\dadd{stable}, or how small their areas made, before the
accuracy goes.
